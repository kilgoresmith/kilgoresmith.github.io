I am engaged in the preparation and translation of a corpus of Himalayan biography, poetry and ritual texts, primarily gathered from the [Buddhist Digital Resource Center (BDRC)](Buddhist Digital Resource Center (BDRC))and the [Nepal National Archives](Nepal National Archives), as well as from direct connections with communities in mountain regions of Nepal. While some of the life writing has been translated and studied by European and American scholars (including UVA’s Kurtis Schaeffer in Himalayan Hermitess 2004; and in [my MA Thesis for Kathmandu University available online](https://ryi.org/ryi-researchers), most of the hundreds (or thousands?) of individual poems remain overlooked by contemporary scholarship. Since Tibetan language religious song (mgur glu / mgur ma) are by nature biographical as well as instructional, by overlooking the narrative element of Himalayan song-poetry, a significant amount of historical information is missed. Additionally, poetry has played a significant role in the transmission of Buddhist values and contemplative practice for millennia, continuing to this day.

Unfortunately, Himalayan students and Tibetan scholars seldom engage with the few English language translations and articles about Himalayan literature currently available, much less the generally educated reader of Tibetan Buddhist literature (whether in English or Tibetan). Some barriers to access are a lack of awareness of the texts, lack of understanding where to acquire the texts, lack of ability to read the varieties of “headless” (u med) calligraphic scripts in which many are written, and the old manuscripts’ lack of standard spellings or editing. These challenges can be overcome by collating available recensions of a work and producing a clean edition with standard spelling and grammar for the general Tibetan language reader; a critical edition in Tibetan for scholars; and a well composed English translation for the international reader, to be published in an accessible online place. 
	One of the primary aims of my interest in Digital Humanities is the potential to make obscure and inaccessible literature available to the public, in addition to our traditional scholarly communities. Specifically, I think of young Tibetan and Himalayan persons living in Nepal and elsewhere who are plugged into online world and social media primarily through mobile phones, and international tourists to the Himalayas interested in the histories and literatures of Himalayan areas they visit. Both use online information accessed via their phones yet have limited access to high quality vetted sources. 
  
On the analysis side, LLM technology presents new opportunities for the rapid survey of textual materials to generate novel research questions, though the ever-present risks for error, bias and inaccurate results currently limit their application. Currently [Dharmamitra](https://www.dharmamitra.org) and [BDRC](https://www.bdrc.io) are working on tools for transcription of handwritten calligraphic Tibetan manuscripts with Handwritten Textual Recognition (HTR), which will be useful for searching a corpus for key terms or phrases allowing us to rapidly identify people, places, ideas, contemplative practices and texts within the corpus. For example, BDRC is releasing [Yigdzin, a free online OCR tool](https://ocr.bdrc.io/uploads) for handwritten and xylographic Tibetan. Though it has potential to aid my research, it is limited since the HTR is nowhere near ready for use in preparation of diplomatic scholarly editions needed for academic translations, which need close to 100% accuracy. The error rate for xylographic scripts is around 3%, and for different calligraphic scripts between 7-11%. These are huge advances good for distant reading applications, and I look forward to their increased accuracy as the models train on more texts.

There are uses other to LLMs in my own work, whether it is organizing information from texts, locating relevant sources for research, or helping unlock the meaning of particularly challenging passages. I’ve been impressed Dharmamitra’s research LLM tool for classical Buddhist textual research, which can translate classical Tibetan reasonably well and can provide intertextual relationships for those passages. Though the environmental resource problems with the energy consumption of the hardware are a real issue, I am comfortable using Dharmamitra sparingly because the organizations behind them are actual educational institutes and non-profits which are unlikely to charge a service fee (and if they do in the future, the money would not be going to enrich the rich at the expense of the rest). 
  
I think that the model is one that should be emulated by a large consortium of educational institutions. Rather than continue to pine and harp about whether or not to use commercial LLMs, academic organizations, which cumulatively have 100s of billions of funds in endowments, should continue to develop free LLMs specifically dedicated for scholarly research and academic writing, such as [Consensus](https://guides.lib.virginia.edu/consensus), available online for us through the UVA library. Some funds should be used to offset the social and environmental harms of the rapid buildout of data centers, power infrastructure, and corresponding increase in emissions, which I will discuss in a later post. For those of us who work on Himalayan material, the discussion around climate change mitigation and adaptation is never far away, since Himalayan communities are already experiencing the brunt of the climate crisis, along with costal communities around the world. Just this week there was [another major glacial collapse and landslide in upper Gorkha, Nepal](https://thehimalayantimes.com/nepal/gorkha-flood-triggered-by-massive-landslide-glacial-debris-in-upper-budhi-gandaki-report-says) which devastated a number of downstream villages. Those communities are facing the realities of relocation and the associated loss of cultural heritage. My own work aims to aims to increase attention to the deep cultural heritage of these areas, which is one of my own small contributions to its preservation and continued development.
