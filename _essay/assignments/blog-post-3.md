title: The impact of generative AI and LLMs on critical making
order: 5

Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson, professors at University of Central Florida, presented a talk and workshop on Oct 8 and 9, titled [“Critical (Un)making and the Agentic Humanities”](https://iah.as.virginia.edu/digital-humanities-lecture-series-emily-johnson-and-anastasia-salter-critical-unmaking-and-agentic) and the workshop [“Critical Making: Grammars, Hypertexts, and Play in the Age of AI”](https://global.virginia.edu/events/digital-humanities-seminarworkshop-emily-johnson-and-anastasia-salter-critical-making). I found aspects of both provocative, as they articulated the recurring problem of “enshittification” with apps and online programs targeted toward professionals, students, and academics. Most of us have experienced the agony of the loss or disfiguration of a loved application or platform due to changes made by revenue maximizing owners. This leads many of us to avoid new applications, especially if we are on a limited budget or tend to be wary of new technologies in general. As they say in India, “old is gold.” 

Essentially, “enshittification,” a term [popularized by Cory Doctorow](https://locusmag.com/feature/commentary-cory-doctorow-social-quitting/),1 is the process by which a new digital app, program or service arcs from gold to shit in four general steps: 1) the new application is “good to its users,” providing a tool or service that enhances their work while charging them little or nothing for its use; 2) the owners “abuse their users” by imposing new fee structures once a critical mass of users has adopted the technology or platform “to make things better for their business customers (which they pay since new fees are perceived as less than the lost productivity or connectivity of abandoning the app or platform, they pay); 3) then they “abuse those business customers” to pull in as much revenue as possible (which they pay since new fees are perceived as less than the lost productivity or connectivity of abandoning the app or platform); and 4) “they die” when the service no longer makes enough to justify the private equity expense. 

We can reasonably expect the developers of popular USA-based LLM chatbots and agentic tools to act similarly with new tools, and in fact have already seen the enshittification arc in progress with the re-incorporation of non-profits as for-profit corporations. The massive (debt-financed) infrastructural rollout that has accompanied the development of new tools and infrastructures indicates expectations of future windfalls from companies, academic institutions, government, and the general public paying to utilize their services. It appears that despite the reticence of many people partly due to concern about environmental, social, labor and intellectual property ethical issues, adoption is increasing rapidly. For example, by Nov 2025, [88% of US companies reported using AI in at least one business function](https://prefactor.tech/learn/ai-agent-adoption-statistics).2

While acknowledging ambivalence about AI supported technologies as they become more difficult to avoid, I was struck by Professor Salter’s encouragement of their use. Though aware of the multiplexes of complications of the adoption of LLMs and agents in our work, she seemed to imply that all of us non-users are doing ourselves and students a disservice for not using Claude and Dots in our work, teaching them to our students, or at least allowing them for assignments. Perhaps I misunderstood her, but I was left feeling like a Luddite as I continue to avoid to use LLMs for personal or professional purposes, much less spend money on them. Administrators or tenured professors may be able to spend $100/month on a premium subscription to Anthropic for Claude and Claude Code while real wages are decreasing, while underpaid grad students or adjunct lecturers may be stretched. They may sabotage their message of using the masters’ tools to build the tools to get out of the walled garden built by reliance on LLMs, ignoring the impending enshittification of those tools.

In a way, the tension between using commercial LLMs or agentic tools in academic work mirrors the “yack” vs. “hack” and “stack” debates. Scholar-teachers who see their jobs as primarily one of teaching students to practice formulating questions through wide reading, discussion and reflection, developing arguments to answer those questions through close reading and analysis, and practicing the skills of composition. The mantra that we have been using for students for years when teaching writing (derided as “the tyranny of the essay”) is that “writing is thinking,” since writing allows the student to methodically craft arguments on paper and revisit them over time as their own thinking develops on an issue. So, are expected to no longer teach students to think? 

Of course I am being hyperbolic on my soapbox, but one middle-way solution to get the benefits of an LLM without *some* of the drawback, not mentioned in the talk, are closed LLMs developed for academic research, such as [Consensus AI](https://library.virginia.edu/news/2026/avoid-ai-hallucinations-try-consensus), available for free via the UVA library. Since it only searches and is trained on articles in academic journals, it doesn’t hallucinate, but more importantly it isn’t there to write for you, but to organize sources and summarize them to make research more efficient. One could use it as a short-cut for making annotated bibliographies, but the annotated bibliography isn’t an end unto itself, it is usually a means to gather, organize and evaluate potential sources for a paper.
Making is a process. As creative beings, we engage the process because it is fun and enriches our lives. It teaches us about ourselves and our place in larger creative conversations, to which we contribute and react. Critical making is being reflective and intentional while engaging in that process, to support a scholarly or activist endeavor. It is a privilege to be able to create without being concerned about a product or output that can be sold or attract clicks to be monetized. That is the beauty and purpose of the academy – to be able to take time to think, try, experiment, fail, and leave projects incomplete. Most of us do not engage in scholarship to become wealthy from our work, but because we want to have the time and space to formulate questions that are meaningful to us and our academic peers. A privilege that seems worth keeping.
