This post is in response to a lecture and workshop held at UVA on September 3 and 4 by Prof. Patricia Murrieta-Flores, who teaches at Tecnológico de Monterrey in Mexico and Lancaster University in the UK. Her lecture, titled “Teaching machines to read the Conquest of America: Unlocking 500 years of real data from the colonial archive with AI” described the current state of her team’s development of novel research tools and methods for transcribing and researching colonial Spanish language documents in the Archivo General de la Nación de México (Mexico’s National Archive), and the workshop challenged participants to apply a decolonial orientation to the conception, design and implementation of humanities scholarship. Professor Murrieta-Flores’ recent work involve creating a calligraphy classifier for sorting the five primary types of handwritten calligraphy used in colonial documents, the development of Handwritten Textual Recognition (HTR) tools for transcribing those five calligraphies, and an online tool for transcribing documents. 

Historical calligraphic scripts of Spanish and other languages can often take years of paleographic training to master to the point where a scholar can accurately transcribe them, and transcription work can be tedious and time consuming (and therefore expensive). These and other barriers (such as lack of geographical or infrastructural access) can prevent students from indigeneous communities engaging with the colonial documents, some of which can be useful in understandings of their own histories, cultures (including *materia medica*) and landscapes; and have proven critical in legal cases challenging land use by mining or forestry corporations. Low barrier tools for colonial document transcription address multiple challenges: while robust historical handwriting transcription tools are available for English, French and Latin (for example, LEO or Transkribus), they are not optimized for colonial Spanish calligraphies, nor are they freely available. While CUNY’s Dominican Studies Initiative is developing a free tool for use on colonial paleographies, it appears underdeveloped (though I haven’t tested it). The hope is that by making transcription tools freely available, students and scholars will be able to widely search the colonial archives and read documents that may be relevant to their work (or legal case), even if they cannot decipher the script in which the document was originally written.
  
This question touches my own work directly, as I spend a great deal of time deciphering handwritten calligraphic Tibetan texts from the Nepal Himalayas, sourced from the [Buddhist Digital Resource Center (BDRC)](https://www.bdrc.io/) and the Nepal National Archives. While texts in the former archive are free for download, available editions are currently limited. Manuscripts at the Nepal National Archives can only be purchased in person in Kathmandu. While many Himalayan and foreign students of Tibetan are learning to read the common “headed” scripts, the calligraphic “headless” scripts in which many of the old manuscripts are written are not studied as widely and take significant time to learn to decipher with accuracy and speed. Therefore, such a tool would be immensely beneficial for scholars interested in distant reading of Himalayan manuscripts, especially for those interested in local histories and peripheral transmissions of Buddhist textual and practice lineages, which are only available in limited numbers of manuscript editions (not to mention the vast amount of religious poetry contained in the biographies and song collections of the Himalayas). Similar machine learning tool (versions of which are under development by teams at BDRC and Dharmamitra.org) would make it possible to convert difficult to read handwritten manuscripts into digitized uni-code fonts that are easily formattable and searchable. The initial barriers for Himalayan, Tibetan and international scholars who do not read the traditional headless scripts would be lowered, so that perhaps more would become interested in reading this traditional un-studied body of literature. That said the transcriptions produced would not be of sufficient fidelity for use for scholarly translation or critical editions, since the prolific regional orthographic idiosyncracies would not be “fixed” or smoothed over by the transcription technologies. That would require an additional set of tools to clean the manuscripts of noise and accurately edit archaic and non-standard spellings into modern Tibetan for the contemporary reader. ![Dharmamitra online tool for OCR, translation, and analysis of Buddhist texts](/assets/img/Dharmamitra_screenshot)

That said, it seems that most young or seasoned Himalayan students or scholars are not aware of the resources on BDRC nor at the Nepal National Archives. Thus, at present it seems primarily aspirational that machine learning can be realized to confront data injustice without the consistent application of existing tools are resources to address unequal access to and development of the human and material resources that are required for engaging with, applying, and publishing decolonizing research.
